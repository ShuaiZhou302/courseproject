\begin{abstract}
Contact-rich, highly dynamic in-hand skills such as pen spinning remain hard to
transfer from simulation to reality. PenSpin~\cite{wang2024penspin} found that
policies trained purely in simulation fail on hardware, and instead fine-tuned
the policy on fewer than 50 successful real-world trajectories collected by
open-loop replay. That data fixes the policy, not the simulator, so any later
training in simulation cannot benefit from it. We propose to spend the same
small replay dataset on correcting the simulator's contact signals instead. We
learn a contact alignment network that maps the training simulator's contact
observations to those measured in the target domain, freeze it inside the
training simulator, re-train the sensorimotor student policy on the aligned
touch signal, and deploy it zero-shot. Because tactile ground truth on real
hardware is expensive and unobservable, we study this in a sim-to-sim setting:
Isaac Gym~\cite{makoviychuk2021isaac} as the training simulator and
SuperDex~\cite{superdex2026}, a contact-focused simulator with native tactile
sensing, as a proxy real world. This setting lets us control the size of the
gap and measure how much of the transfer gap comes from tactile observations
versus contact dynamics.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Dexterous Manipulation, Sim-to-Real Transfer,
Tactile Sensing, System Identification

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning in simulation is the main way robots acquire dexterous
in-hand skills that humans cannot easily
teleoperate~\cite{qi2022hora,openai2020dexterous,qi2023general}. Pen spinning is
an extreme case: it is fast, contact-rich and unforgiving of small errors.
PenSpin~\cite{wang2024penspin} reports that extensive domain randomization,
hardware iteration and different input modalities all failed to transfer, and
that the pure physics gap remained large even after isolating touch and vision.

PenSpin's fix was to replay the simulated oracle open-loop on hardware, keep the
successful trajectories, and fine-tune the student policy on them. This works
with very little data, but it treats real data as demonstrations for one policy.
The simulator stays wrong, so the policy cannot keep improving in simulation,
and a new task or policy needs new real data.

We take the opposite view: use the small target-domain dataset to correct the
simulator, then keep training in simulation. This follows a line of work that
adapts simulation from real
experience~\cite{chebotar2019closing,he2025asap,ramos2019bayessim}, including
recent tactile calibration by replaying joint trajectories~\cite{real2sim2real2026}.
We focus on the contact signal, because it is both a policy input and the place
where simulators disagree most for this task.

We evaluate in a sim-to-sim setting, with SuperDex~\cite{superdex2026} standing
in for the real world. SuperDex models non-convex collisions, distributed
contact forces, soft contact and tactile sensors natively, so its contact
behavior differs from Isaac Gym's in the way we care about. The setting gives us
unlimited evaluation, ground-truth contact, and a gap whose size we can tune. We
treat it as a controlled proxy study and do not claim real-world results.

We ask three questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:},leftmargin=*]
  \item How much of the transfer gap comes from tactile observations, and how
        much from contact dynamics?
  \item How much zero-shot performance does a contact alignment network,
        trained on a few replayed trajectories, recover?
  \item How does correcting the simulator compare with PenSpin-style policy
        fine-tuning at the same data budget?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}

We keep PenSpin's first two stages unchanged and replace its real-world
fine-tuning with a learned correction of the simulator's contact signal. We call
Isaac Gym the source domain $\src$ and SuperDex the target domain $\tgt$
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\paragraph{Setup.} An Allegro hand rotates pen-like objects, built on PenSpin's
released code. The contact observation $c \in \{0,1\}^K$ is a binary vector over
the $K$ hand links that touch the object. In $\src$ it comes from thresholded
contact forces; in $\tgt$ we pool SuperDex's native tactile readings onto the
same $K$ links.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/pipeline}
\caption{Pipeline. All policy training stays in Isaac Gym; SuperDex only
supplies replay data for the contact alignment network $f$ and hosts the final
zero-shot test.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Oracle.} Train an oracle policy with PPO in $\src$ using
        privileged object state and physical parameters, as in PenSpin.
  \item \textbf{Student.} Distill a sensorimotor student whose input is a
        history of proprioception $q$ and contacts $c_\src$.
  \item \textbf{Replay and collect.} Replay oracle action sequences open-loop in
        $\tgt$ from matched initial states. Record target contacts $c_\tgt$
        alongside $c_\src$, $q$ and actions $a$. We keep all rollouts: the
        success label filters data for the PenSpin baseline, not for the sensor
        model, since failed rollouts still carry valid contact readings.
  \item \textbf{Contact alignment.} Train a small temporal network $f$ that
        predicts $c_\tgt$ at time $t$ from a window of $(c_\src, q, a)$:
        \begin{equation}
          \hat{c}_{\tgt,t} = f_\phi\big(c_{\src,t-H:t},\, q_{t-H:t},\, a_{t-H:t}\big),
          \qquad
          \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathrm{BCE}\big(\hat{c}_{\tgt,t},\, c_{\tgt,t}\big).
        \end{equation}
        Conditioning on $q$ and $a$ matters because replayed trajectories drift
        apart in $\src$ and $\tgt$, so contacts at the same step are not from
        identical states.
  \item \textbf{Re-distill.} Freeze $f$ inside $\src$, feed the student
        $f(c_\src)$ instead of $c_\src$, and distill again from the fixed oracle.
  \item \textbf{Deploy.} Run the re-distilled student zero-shot in $\tgt$ on
        SuperDex's native contacts.
\end{enumerate}

$\tgt$ also allows a pairing that hardware does not: reset $\tgt$ to the exact
state of $\src$ at each step and take one step. This yields state-matched
contact pairs, which we use only as an upper bound for $f$.

states. Every method that uses target data gets the same replay budget

\paragraph{Metrics.} Full rotations per episode and time before the pen drops,
following PenSpin; contact prediction F1 of $f$ on held-out target rollouts.

\paragraph{Data efficiency (RQ2, RQ3).} Repeat the comparison with 10, 25, 50
and 100 replayed trajectories.

\paragraph{Gap decomposition (RQ1).} The proprio-only student's drop from
$\src$ to $\tgt$ estimates the dynamics gap alone. The tactile student's drop
inside $\src$ when its contacts pass through the frozen $f$ estimates the
observation gap alone. We also vary SuperDex friction and contact compliance to
see how both terms grow with the size of the gap.

\section{Plan}

The largest risk is porting the environment to SuperDex, so it comes first after
the baseline reproduction.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{10/09/2026 -- 10/23/2026:} Reproduce PenSpin's oracle and
        student in Isaac Gym from the released code; fix the shared $K$-link
        contact representation.
  \item \textbf{10/24/2026 -- 11/06/2026:} Port the Allegro hand, pen objects and
        PD controller to SuperDex and check that controls match; run open-loop
        replay and collect paired contact data.
  \item \textbf{11/07/2026 -- 11/20/2026:} Train the contact alignment network,
        re-distill the student, and get first zero-shot results.
  \item \textbf{11/21/2026 -- 12/04/2026:} Run baselines, data-efficiency and
        gap-decomposition experiments.
  \item \textbf{12/05/2026 -- 12/11/2026:} Summarize results in the final report
        and presentation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Fallbacks.} If replay in SuperDex almost never succeeds, we start from
an easier in-hand rotation task~\cite{qi2022hora} before pens. If porting stalls,
we use MuJoCo with soft-contact settings as the target domain. If the gap turns
out too small, we widen it through SuperDex's friction and compliance
parameters.
